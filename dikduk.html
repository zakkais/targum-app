Dikduk

A short grammar of Hebrew and Aramaic, following the classical works: the Radak and the Ramchal for Hebrew, and Yitzhak Frank for Aramaic. Tap any example word to open it in the dictionary. See the Sources page for the works this section is based on.

The Shoresh (Root)

Most Hebrew words are built on a root of three letters, the שורש. The root letters carry the core meaning, and different patterns built around them make verbs, nouns, and adjectives. Once you know the root, you can recognize a whole family of words.

RootCore meaningWords from the root
כ־ת־בwriteכָּתַב (he wrote), כְּתָב (writing), מִכְתָּב (letter), כְּתֹבֶת (address)
ש־מ־רguardשָׁמַר (he guarded), שׁוֹמֵר (guard), מִשְׁמָר (watch, guard duty)
ס־פ־רcount; tellסָפַר (he counted), סוֹפֵר (scribe), סֵפֶר (book), מִסְפָּר (number)

The Radak's ספר השרשים (Sefer HaShorashim) is arranged by root: every word is listed under its three root letters, with the verb forms divided by binyan.

PatternWhat it makesExample

קָטִילAn adjectiveחָסִיד — pious; תָּמִים — whole, upright
מִקְטָלA place or instrumentמִכְתָּב — letter; מִשְׁכָּן — dwelling, Tabernacle

Gender and Number

Every Hebrew noun is masculine or feminine. Feminine nouns often end in ־ָה or ־ֶת. Plurals usually end in ־ִים for masculine and ־וֹת for feminine, and pairs take the dual ending ־ַיִם.

FormEndingExample
Masculine plural־ִיםסוּס (horse) → סוּסִים
Feminine plural־וֹתסוּסָה (mare) → סוּסוֹת
Dual (two, or a natural pair)־ַיִםיוֹם (day) → יוֹמַיִם (two days); עַיִן (eye) → עֵינַיִם (eyes)

Some nouns break the pattern: אָבוֹת (fathers) is masculine but takes ־וֹת, and מִלִּים (words) is feminine but takes ־ִים. The ending alone does not always tell you the gender.

Prefixes and the Definite Article

Several one-letter words attach to the front of a Hebrew word: the definite article הַ־ (the), the prepositions בְּ־ (in), כְּ־ (like), לְ־ (to), מִ־ (from), the conjunction וְ־ (and), and the relative שֶׁ־ (that). The Ramchal's ספר הדקדוק (Sefer HaDikduk) teaches grammar through the letters themselves: the roles each letter plays as a root letter, as a prefix, or as a suffix.

PrefixMeaningExample
הַ־theהַסֵּפֶר — the book
בְּ־in, withבְּסֵפֶר — in a book
כְּ־like, asכְּסֵפֶר — like a book
לְ־to, forלְסֵפֶר — to a book
מִ־fromמִסֵּפֶר — from a book
וְ־andוְסֵפֶר — and a book
שֶׁ־that, whichשֶׁסֵּפֶר — that a book

Noun States

An Aramaic noun appears in three states. The absolute is the plain form, the construct joins the noun to the word after it ("king of..."), and the emphatic ends in ־ָא and usually means "the". In the Gemara the emphatic state is the ordinary form of the noun, and often carries no special "the" force.

StateExampleMeaning
Absoluteמֶלֶךְa king
Constructמָלֵךְking of (e.g. מָלֵךְ מַלְכַיָּא, king of kings)
Emphaticמַלְכָּאthe king
Absoluteבַּרa son
Emphaticבְּרָאthe son

Plurals

Aramaic plurals end in ־ִין for masculine and ־ָן for feminine. The emphatic plural ends in ־ַיָּא.

The Verb Stems

Like Hebrew, Aramaic verbs come in stems, each with its own meaning. The six stems are shown here with the root כ־ת־ב (write).

StemMeaningExample
פְּעַל (Peal)Simple activeכְּתַב — he wrote
פַּעֵל (Pael)Intensive activeכַּתֵּב — he wrote out, inscribed
אַפְעֵל (Afel)Causativeאַכְתֵּב — he caused to write
אִתְפְּעֵל (Itpeel)Passive or reflexive of Pealאִתְכְּתֵב — it was written
אִתְפַּעַל (Itpaal)Reflexive of Paelאִתְכַּתַּב — he had himself inscribed
אִתַּפְעַל (Ittafal)Passive of Afelאִתַּכְתֵּב — he was made to write

The Participle as Present Tense

Aramaic has no separate present tense: the participle does the job. כָּתֵב means "he writes" or "he is writing"; אָזֵיל means "he goes"; אָתֵי means "he comes". The particle קָא before a participle marks an action in progress: קָא כָּתֵב — he is writing right now.

Pronominal Suffixes

Pronouns attach to the end of nouns and prepositions. With בְּר־ (son):

SuffixExampleMeaning
־ִיבְּרִיmy son
־ָךְבְּרָךְyour son
־ֵיהּבְּרֵיהּhis son
־ָהּבְּרָהּher son
־ַןבְּרַןour son
־ְכוֹןבְּרְכוֹןyour (plural) son
־ְהוֹןבְּרְהוֹןtheir son

The same suffixes appear on prepositions: לֵיהּ means "to him", so אָמַר לֵיהּ is "he said to him". דִּילֵיהּ means "his" — literally, "of him".
